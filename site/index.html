Maya: an offline AI travel aid for blind and low-vision people

Maya runs on a laptop with a camera at chest height and a pair of headphones. It watches the sidewalk and listens to the street. When something matters, you hear a beep that pans left or right and speeds up as it gets closer, or a few words like "Person left, 4." or "Truck backing up." Every model runs on the laptop, so there is no cloud, no account and no need for a signal. Maya tells you what is around you. It never tells you where to go.

Use headphones: beeps move left and right.

What Maya says in this clip
  1. 0.7 s Curb and crosswalk ahead, 5.
  2. 3.8 s Car ahead, close.
  3. 5.2 s Cars passing.
  4. 6.1 s Person ahead, 6.
  5. 10.2 s Person crossing ahead, close.
  6. 13.3 s Hydrant ahead, 9.

A number is a distance in steps. "Close" means very near.

Who it is for

Maya is for blind and low-vision people who walk on their own with a long cane or a guide dog. Most people with vision loss still have some sight (AFB), so Maya is built for people with partial sight as well as for people who are totally blind.

Main causes

Among adults 50 and over, the main causes of blindness in 2020 were cataract (15.2 million people), glaucoma (3.6 million), age-related macular degeneration (1.8 million) and diabetic retinopathy (0.86 million) (GBD 2019, Lancet Global Health). Each takes sight in a different way. Glaucoma and retinitis pigmentosa take side vision first. Macular degeneration takes the centre and leaves the sides (NEI).

Who gains most

  • People with side vision loss from glaucoma or retinitis pigmentosa. People with glaucoma bump into obstacles more often than sighted people (PMC6058912). Beeps that pan left and right point at the side they miss.
  • People with hemianopia after a stroke, who lose the same half of the field in both eyes (PMC4181645). A cue from one side fits this well.
  • Older adults. Vision loss rises steeply with age (CDC VEHSS), and falls are more common with it. People who lose sight later in life may never have had mobility training, so short spoken cues may help them most.
  • Cane users. A cane finds things at ground level but misses signs, branches and overhangs at chest and head height. Maya's camera sees the whole frame and names what is there, like "Sign board ahead."
  • Guide dog users. The dog already steers around obstacles. What Maya adds is names: a dog can avoid a sign board but cannot say what it is.

Fully local, open models

Maya uses no cloud service and needs no account. Camera images never leave the laptop and nothing is stored. Every model has open weights and runs on the laptop itself, on one 8 GB GPU and the CPU.

Privacy for the user

Blind people who use camera apps worry about sending out images they cannot check first (Akter et al., USENIX Security 2020). With Maya, those images stay on the laptop.

Privacy for people nearby

A walking camera sees strangers. In surveys of 128 visually impaired users and 136 sighted bystanders, both groups had concerns about camera-based aids capturing and describing people nearby (Akter et al., ASSETS 2020 and TACCESS 2022). Maya keeps each frame in memory on the laptop and then discards it. The camera still sees people's faces, even though nothing is kept.

Works with no signal

GPS aids already fail in stations and indoors (PMC10691587). About 2.2 billion people are still offline, and in rural areas 58% are online against 85% in cities (ITU, 2025). An aid that depends on the cloud stops working in those places.

No round trip

US signal timing assumes people walk about 1.07 m per second (NYMTC, 2012). Each second spent waiting on a server is roughly a metre walked before the warning arrives. Maya's detector and rules respond on the next frame.

No subscription

A human describer service like Aira starts at $26 a month for 20 minutes (Aira pricing, 2026). Maya costs nothing per use once installed. It does need a laptop with a capable GPU, which costs money up front.

How it works

Maya is a pipeline of small models. Each one has a single job. A rule engine decides what you hear, so a model never speaks to you directly.

NVIDIA Cosmos3-Edge, 4-bit ubr-physical-ai/Cosmos3-Edge-NF4-bnb Walking lane check once a second, crosswalk confirm OpenMDW-1.1 (see model card)
Crosswalk stripe detector (our code on OpenCV) Painted crosswalk stripes ahead, from pixels alone OpenCV: Apache-2.0
Audio Spectrogram Transformer MIT/ast-finetuned-audioset-10-10-0.4593 Hears sirens, horns and reversing trucks BSD-3-Clause
Piper TTS, en_US-lessac-medium voice Speaks every word in one voice, on the CPU GPL-3.0-or-later (engine). Voice: see model card.

Outside walk mode, the same app answers spoken questions about a photo, a sign, a document, a sound or a short video. It uses Qwen3-VL-4B (Apache-2.0), also on the laptop.

What worked and what didn't

For each version, an AI agent checked every spoken line against the video frames from 1 second before to 1 second after it, on the same 22 clips.

Worked

  • Step counts. Distances like "Person left, 5." were about right in 40 of 49 checks.
  • Naming obstacles the detector had already found, such as a scissor lift, a planter and a sign board.
  • The stripe curb cue. It fired within about 2 seconds of the curb at 9 of 11 held-out corners and stayed silent on 6 clips with no crossing.
  • Sound alerts for sirens, horns and reversing trucks, from the sound alone.
  • Staying quiet. The first combined version spoke 164 lines on the review clips, and 38 of them were wrong. The version that fits the live GPU budget spoke 114, with 8 wrong and 4 late. Of 45 people walking toward the camera in our notes, 38 got a beep or a word between 2 seconds before and 1 second after the moment we noted.

Didn't work

  • Cosmos steering. In frame reviews, 1 of its 15 lines was useful, 6 were wrong and 8 came too late. Some would have stepped the walker into a person, so it is off.
  • Cosmos free descriptions were useful 2 times in 25.
  • Stairs. 0 of 7 stair alerts were right, so that check is off too.
  • Crosswalk warnings come late, only about 4 steps ahead, with some false alarms.

Limits

  • An aid alongside the cane or guide dog, never a replacement. Keep using your cane or dog. Maya only adds information.
  • Not a medical device. It does not diagnose, treat or measure vision, and has no regulatory clearance. It is a hackathon prototype.
  • Describes, never steers. Maya says "Person ahead." It never says "Move left" or "Safe to cross."
  • Audio only. As built, it does not work for deafblind people. Beeps and words can also compete with traffic sounds, so open-ear headphones are a better fit.
