### 1.2 Leading causes

Global causes of blindness in adults 50 and over (2020): cataract 15.2 million, glaucoma 3.6 million, age-related macular degeneration 1.8 million, diabetic retinopathy 0.86 million. Source: GBD 2019 Blindness and Vision Impairment Collaborators, *Lancet Global Health* 9:e144 to e160, 2021, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7820391

Distance impairment or blindness by cause (WHO 2026 fact sheet): cataract 94 million, refractive error 88.4 million, AMD 8 million, glaucoma 7.7 million, diabetic retinopathy 3.9 million.

In the US, NEI names diabetic retinopathy, AMD, cataract and glaucoma as the leading causes (NEI, https://www.nei.nih.gov/about/news-and-events/news/vision-loss-eye-diseases-will-increase-americans-age).

| Cause | What it does to sight | Relevance to Maya |
|---|---|---|
| Cataract | clouded lens, blurry and glare-sensitive vision; usually treatable by surgery | large numbers but often curable; helps people waiting for or without access to surgery |
| Glaucoma | optic nerve damage, peripheral field lost first, often unnoticed until advanced (NEI) | strong fit: side objects are missed, which panned left/right beeps address |
| AMD | central vision lost, side vision kept (NEI) | sign reading, "what is ahead" lane checks and image Q&A help most; obstacle beeps less critical |
| Diabetic retinopathy | patchy loss, blur, macular edema affects central vision (NEI) | mixed central and patchy loss; description and Q&A modes |
| Retinitis pigmentosa | night vision lost first, then field narrows to tunnel vision; rare, roughly 1 in 3,000 to 1 in 5,000 (AAO EyeWiki, https://eyewiki.aao.org/Retinitis_Pigmentosa; NEI, https://www.nei.nih.gov/learn-about-eye-health/eye-conditions-and-diseases/retinitis-pigmentosa) | strong fit: tunnel vision plus poor night vision is the classic case for side-object and low-light cues |

### 1.3 Which groups gain most from a walking aid like this

- Low vision, not total blindness, is the majority. AFB notes most people with blindness retain some vision (https://afb.org/blindness-and-low-vision/eye-conditions/low-vision-and-legal-blindness-terms-and-descriptions). One commonly cited figure is that only about 10 to 20 percent of people who meet the legal definition are totally blind (Psychology Today, 2024, https://www.psychologytoday.com/au/blog/blindness-visible/202408/how-can-you-be-blind-if-you-still-can-see); treat that as an approximation. Maya should be pitched at people with some remaining sight as well as totally blind travelers.
- People with peripheral field loss (glaucoma, RP). Studies find people with glaucoma contact obstacles more often than sighted controls, especially when multitasking (PMC6058912, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6058912), and that glaucoma is an independent risk factor for falls (review, PMC3960394, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3960394). People with more field loss also travel away from home less (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04396002). Left/right panned beeps target exactly what this group misses.
- People with hemianopia after stroke. Homonymous hemianopia (loss of the same half of the field in both eyes) is most often caused by stroke in adults (PMC4181645, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4181645). A one-sided cue system fits this group well. This is an inference, not a tested claim.
- Older adults with age-related loss. Vision loss rises steeply with age (CDC VEHSS 2024, see table) and fall rates nearly double with severe impairment (CDC MMWR 2016). Older adults may be newer to vision loss and less likely to have completed O&M training, so a simple spoken cue may matter more to them.
- Cane users. The long cane finds ground-level obstacles but misses things at chest and head height (overhangs, signs, branches); that gap is why products like WeWALK add an upward ultrasonic sensor (RNIB product listing, https://shop.rnib.org.uk/products/wewalk-v2-smart-cane-grey; Romlay et al. review cited in search). Maya's camera sees the whole frame, including head-height objects, and adds names ("Sign board ahead").
- Guide-dog users. A dog already steers around obstacles, so beeps add less. The gain is in naming things and reading signs, which a dog cannot do. Guide dog use is a small share of blind people; a frequently repeated figure is about 2%, but NFB notes reliable current data are scarce (https://nfb.org/node/3757). Do not cite 2% as fact.
- Deafblind users: audio cues fail. WFDB estimates deafblind people are between 0.2% and 2% of the population, with most among older people (WFDB Global Report, 2018, https://wfdb.eu/deafblindness-in-the-world/). Maya is audio-only today (beeps plus Piper TTS), so it does not serve this group. Haptic output (vibration, or a refreshable braille display) is the obvious extension; worth stating as a known gap.
- Note on hearing. Blind travelers rely on hearing traffic. Beeps and speech should not mask street sound (open-ear or bone-conduction audio, short words, sparse cues). This is a design consideration rather than a sourced number.

### Community language preferences

- "Blind" and "low vision" are the safe, plain words. AFB's reporter guide says blind, legally blind and low vision are all acceptable depending on context, and asks writers to avoid "the blind" as a group noun, pity words like "suffers from", and euphemisms like "hard of seeing" (AFB, https://www.afb.org/press-room/reporter-resources-writing-about-blindness-vision-loss).
- "Visually impaired" is contested. The National Federation of the Blind has opposed it as a euphemism since Resolution 93-01 (1993) and passed further resolutions in July 2024 asking media and agencies to use "blind" or "low vision" instead (NFB 2024 Resolutions, https://nfb.org/node/15858; full text not fetched, 403). CNIB also advises against it unless a person chooses it (CNIB Inclusive Language Guidelines, 2024, https://cnib.ca/sites/default/files/2024-10/24-0432-CNIB_Inclusive%20Language%20Guidelines_ENG_FINAL-s.pdf).
- Person-first versus identity-first. Both are used. NFB and many blind advocates use identity-first ("blind people"); AFB and CNIB default to person-first ("person with low vision") when unsure. Practical choice for Maya: "blind and low-vision people" in headlines (short, identity-first, accepted by NFB), and "people with vision loss" where you mean the whole spectrum including older adults who do not call themselves blind.
- Avoid "vision difficulty". It is the US Census American Community Survey self-report item (blind, or serious difficulty seeing even with glasses), not a clinical or community term (CDC, https://www.cdc.gov/vision-health-data/data-sources/american-community-survey.html).

## 3. Framing

### Judging "Local AI assisted device for people with vision difficulty"

- "Device" is inaccurate: Maya is software on a laptop, not dedicated hardware.
- "Local" is ambiguous (local community? local shop?). "On-device" or "offline" says what you mean.
- "AI assisted device" is backwards: the person is assisted, not the device. It also needs a hyphen ("AI-assisted").
- "Vision difficulty" is a census survey term, not what clinicians or the community say (see section 2).
- It does not say what Maya does (walking cues, describing, answering) or why local matters (privacy, no signal).

### Candidate taglines

1. Maya: an offline AI travel aid for blind and low-vision people.
2. Hears the street, sees the path, speaks the hazards. Fully on-device.
3. Private, on-device scene and hazard cues for people with vision loss. No cloud, no signal needed.
4. An electronic travel aid that runs on your own GPU: obstacle beeps, sign reading and siren alerts, offline.
5. Your camera stays yours. Local multimodal AI that describes, answers and warns for blind and low-vision users.

### Recommendation

"Maya: an offline AI travel aid for blind and low-vision people."

It uses the community's own words, names the category judges and O&M specialists recognize (travel aid, a plain form of ETA), and leads with the single property that separates Maya from Be My AI, Seeing AI scene descriptions, Aira and WeWALK's assistant: it works with no network. Use tagline 3 as the subtitle to carry privacy and "people with vision loss" for the broader audience.

## 4. Why local / on-device matters for this group

- Privacy for the user. Blind users of camera apps worry about sharing images they cannot check themselves (Akter et al., "I am uncomfortable sharing what I can't see", USENIX Security 2020, https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity20/presentation/akter). Be My AI sends images to a third-party AI provider, needs internet, and keeps descriptions for 30 days by default (Be My Eyes support FAQ, quoted in AppleVis threads 2024 to 2025, https://applevis.com/comment/190125; official page returned 403, so verify before citing).
- Privacy for bystanders. An always-on walking camera records strangers' faces. Surveys of 128 visually impaired users and 136 sighted bystanders found shared concerns about camera-based assistive tech capturing and misdescribing bystanders (Akter et al., ASSETS 2020 and ACM TACCESS 2022, https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/shared-privacy-concerns-of-the-visually-impaired-and-sighted-bystanders-with-camera-based-assistive-technologies/). Keeping every frame on the laptop and never storing it answers this directly.
- No signal. GPS-based aids already fail in stations and indoors (RouteNav transit hub study, PMC10691587, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10691587/). Globally about 2.2 billion people are still offline, and in rural areas 58% are online versus 85% in urban areas (ITU Facts and Figures 2025, https://www.itu.int/itu-d/reports/statistics/facts-figures-2025/; urban/rural split via UN DESA summary). A cloud model goes silent exactly where a traveler may need it.
- Latency for safety cues. US signal timing assumes pedestrians walk about 3.5 ft/s (about 1.07 m/s), with 3.0 ft/s recommended for slower or older pedestrians (MUTCD guidance as summarized by NYMTC fact sheet 2012, https://www.nymtc.org/portals/0/pdf/Transportation%20Safety/NYSAMPO%20TrafficSignalTimingFactSheet_2012.pdf; TCRP/NCHRP recommendation). So each second of round-trip delay is roughly one metre walked before the warning arrives. Local detection plus rules gives a cue within one frame; no network jitter. (No source found giving measured cloud VLM latency for these apps; keep this as arithmetic, not a benchmark.)
- No subscription. Aira's individual plans run from $26 a month for 20 minutes up to $1,160 for 880 minutes (Aira pricing page, updated March 2026, https://www.aira.io/offset-prices). WeWALK's AI voice assistant is a paid subscription in the UK at GBP 4.99 a month after a one-month trial (RNIB shop listing, https://shop.rnib.org.uk/products/wewalk-smart-cane-2-ai). Maya has no per-use cost once installed. Be honest that it needs a laptop with a capable GPU, which is its own cost.

Where Maya differs: it combines obstacle distance beeps (like WeWALK), object and sign naming (like Seeing AI and Be My AI), crosswalk confirmation and sound-event alerts (sirens, horns, reversing trucks) in one pipeline that runs fully offline. ## 6. Honest limits to state

- An aid alongside the cane or guide dog, never a replacement. The cane and dog remain the primary mobility tools (Hersh, 2022). Even WeWALK, itself a cane, tells buyers to learn white cane skills first (RNIB listing). Maya should say plainly: keep using your cane or dog; Maya adds information.
- Not a medical device. It does not diagnose, treat or measure vision, and has no regulatory clearance. It is a hackathon prototype.
- Not yet validated with blind or low-vision users. No user study, no O&M specialist review, no measured miss rate for curbs, drop-offs, stairs or moving vehicles. Detections can be wrong or late, especially at night, in rain or glare.
- Does not guide or steer. Walk mode describes; it does not give turn directions or route guidance (steering was tested and rejected).
- Audio only. Not usable by deafblind people as built; cues may compete with traffic sounds sighted pedestrians never think about.
- Hardware. Needs a laptop with a capable GPU and a camera worn or held at chest height; not yet a phone or wearable.
- Bystander faces. Frames stay on the device and are not stored, but the camera still sees people; say this openly.
